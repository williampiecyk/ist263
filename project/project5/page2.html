Info

The Colosseum, originally known as the Flavian Amphitheatre, was commissioned by Emperor Vespasian around 70-72 AD and completed by his son Titus in 80 AD. Built in the heart of Rome, it stood as the largest amphitheater ever constructed, capable of holding an estimated 50,000 to 80,000 spectators.

Constructed primarily from concrete and stone, the Colosseum showcased remarkable Roman engineering, featuring a complex system of vaults, arches, and passageways that allowed for efficient crowd movement — a design that still influences stadium architecture today. Beneath the arena floor lay the hypogeum, an underground network of tunnels and chambers used to house gladiators, animals, and elaborate stage machinery for dramatic entrances.

For nearly 400 years, the Colosseum hosted a variety of public spectacles, including gladiatorial combats, animal hunts, public executions, and even mock naval battles. These events served both as entertainment and as a display of Roman power and grandeur.

The arena gradually fell out of use by the 6th century, and over the centuries it suffered damage from earthquakes, stone-robbing, and neglect. Despite this, it remained a powerful symbol of Rome.

Today, the Colosseum is one of the world's most iconic landmarks and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, drawing millions of visitors each year who come to marvel at this enduring monument to ancient Roman civilization.
